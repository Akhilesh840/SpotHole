[008] (p  |Abstract       ): Abstract—Potholes damage cars and cause accidents, but we usually rely on slow, pricey manual surveys to find them. Smartphone accelerometers offer a cheaper, crowdsourced way to track road conditions, yet inertial sensors often give false alarms when a driver hits a speed breaker, brakes hard, or steers suddenly. This paper introduces SpotHole, an energy-efficient, two-stage Android framework designed for smartphones mounted on motorcycle handlebars. During the first stage, we project high-frequency (100 Hz) inertial streams onto the earth-relative gravity vector. This creates orientation-invariant vertical acceleration, which we then check using a multi-heuristic consensus rule and kinematic false-positive filters. To stop false triggers without the battery drain or heat of constant computer vision, the second stage runs an on-demand visual check. This uses a lightweight one-class YOLOv8n model (3.01M parameters). We trained the model on 2,046 images containing 7,198 annotated potholes. On 528 validation images, it hits a precision of 0.825, a recall of 0.699, an F1 score of 0.756, and an mAP@0.5 of 0.799 (mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.516). Synthetic degradation tests show the detection holds up well against JPEG compression (ΔmAP < 0.02) and moderate noise. However, performance drops off under severe motion blur (ΔmAP = −0.457) and shifts in ambient lighting (ΔmAP up to −0.260). These results show that vehicle vibration blur and dynamic exposure are the main hurdles for edge vision on two-wheelers, and they provide clear design guidelines for real-world use.

[010] (p  |Heading1       ): Introduction
[011] (p  |BodyText       ): Potholes and unexpected pavement depressions cause a lot of damage to vehicle tires, rims and suspension systems, and represent a serious safety risk to motorists. Two-wheeler and motorcycle riders are particularly vulnerable to such hazards as they have no physical protection and are at increased risk for losing control or fatal collision upon hitting road depressions. Official statistics published by Ministry of Road Transport and Highways (MoRTH) reveal that in India alone, thousands of road crashes and causalities every year are directly caused by potholes/neglected craters with more than 44% of the traffic casualties caused by two-wheelers. Typically, municipal road maintenance departments identify anomalies on roads by conducting regular road audits or through a citizen complaint that is collected retroactively. Both are reactive, labor intensive, and do not detect rapid development of acute structural damage between inspections. High accuracy surface mapping is possible with specialized inspection vehicles carrying automated laser road profilers or high-definition stereoscopic cameras, but the cost of acquisition and operation of these systems is too high for widespread deployment in large fleets operating on vast road networks in urban and rural areas.
[012] (p  |BodyText       ): To resolve this trade-off, this paper presents SpotHole, an energy-efficient Android sensing framework for handlebar-mounted smartphones. In the first stage, inertial sensors sampled at 100 Hz isolate orientation-invariant vertical acceleration via gravity-vector projection, evaluating candidate anomalies through a multi-heuristic consensus rule and kinematic rejection filters. In the second stage, an on-demand verification check runs a lightweight one-class YOLOv8n model (3.01M parameters) only when candidate shocks occur.
[013] (p  |BodyText       ): The main contributions of this work are as follows:
[014] (p  |BodyText       ): An Asymmetric Two-Stage Edge Sensing Framework: We implement SpotHole on Android, combining continuous orientation-invariant inertial screening with on-demand edge computer vision on a handlebar-mounted smartphone. We evaluate a compact YOLOv8n model on a unified multi-source dataset consisting of 2,046 training images (7,198 pothole instances) and 528 validation images.
[015] (p  |BodyText       ): Comprehensive Robustness and Split-Contamination Audit: We examine the robustness of models with synthetic physical degradations (Gaussian blur, Vehicle vibration motion blur, Illumination extremes, Gaussian noise, JPEG compression). We also perform a perceptual hash audit which indicates that there are 242 near-duplicate images shared both within and across common open-source road vision benchmark sets to give a fair baseline of the reproducibility of road vision
[016] (p  |Heading1       ): RELATED WORK
[017] (p  |None           ): 354457014033500%0%A. Accelerometer-Based Detection Previous participatory sensing approaches showed that the anomalies in the road surface at the city scale can be detected from vehicle vibrations. The Pothole Patrol system has been developed using taxi-mounted triaxial accelerometers and GPS, which successfully identified 75% of road anomalies with a false positive rate of less than 5% [1]. Nericell moved sensor data acquisition to consumer smartphones and proposes virtual reorientation algorithms to decouple sensor tilt from vehicle dynamics [2]. Mednis et al. introduced a new set of basic real-time tests based on heuristic threshold algorithms: Z-THRESH, Z-DIFF, STDEV(Z), and G-ZERO [3]. These heuristics are used as common comparators and generalized in later multi-feature inertial fusion research [4] and [5]. Integrating accelerometer and gyroscope streams, Allouch et al. (Road Sense) came up with a classification of anomalies on two-wheelers [6]. The fixed thresholds are vulnerable to vehicle suspension changes, speed of transport and mounting angles, however, the inertial methods are computation light and maintain privacy.
[018] (p  |None           ): 
[019] (p  |None           ): B. Vision-Based Detection In an inertial system, the ambiguity is eliminated by directly measuring the road morphology using the camera. Maeda et al. made a dataset of 9,053 smartphone images for edge inference benchmarking to cloud servers [8]. This work was extended to the multi-national RDD2020 benchmark with more than 26,000 road images with annotations [9]. Near real-time mobile inference is possible with single-stage convolutional detectors, especially YOLO detectors [10] and lightweight YOLOv8 detectors [11]. Outdoor road imagery however suffers significantly from varying ambient illuminations, the presence of shadows and motion blur due to vibration of the vehicles [12] [13]. In addition, constantly using deep networks on mobile devices leads to fast thermal throttling and battery depletion.
[020] (p  |None           ): C. Multi-Modal Sensor and Camera Fusion
[021] (p  |None           ): Recent studies have integrated the two modalities (inertial and visual) to take advantage of each strength. Xin et al. proposed a crowdsourcing pipeline which projected the acceleration from a smartphone and the video vectors in a car into a common embedding space and classified the events by an LSTM network [14]. The combination of an SSD-MobileNet vision detector and an artificial neural network in triaxial IMU data was adopted by Silvister et al. to cross-validate candidate shocks [15]. Likewise, Villareal and Samaniego created an app for Android that combines YOLO object detection with IMU and GPS tracking to achieve a verification accuracy of 64.6% in 18.97 km of testing in the field [16].

[024] (p  |Heading1       ): System Architecture
[025] (p  |Heading2       ): Architectural Overview
[026] (p  |None           ): The Model-View-View Model (MVVM) architectural pattern is used for SpotHole, which is divided into the four functional tiers of sensing, detection, persistence and presentation (see Fig. 1). Smartphone based road monitoring is a continuous process during travel; therefore, the sensing and detection pipelines are running within a persistent Android Foreground Service. This lifecycle mode will not kill the OS background processes when the smartphone is attached to the handlebars of a vehicle. The service performs asynchronous sensor sampling, maintains an in-memory video ring buffer, processes physical and visual candidate triggers and validates anomalies and commits them to a local SQLite/Room database. The presentation layer watches reactive database streams and displays a cluster of anomalies on an interactive map.

[028] (p  |Heading2       ): Sensing Layer
[029] (p  |Heading2       ): Four smartphone sensors are accessed by the service using the Android SensorManager: the accelerometer, the gyroscope, the linear acceleration sensor and the gravity sensor. The application wants to sample at 100 Hz (a 10,000 microsecond delay hint). As with Android's non-deterministic delivery of hardware sensors, our field tests revealed an effective measured average rate of 67.7 Hz (average interval of 14.77 milliseconds). The tilting effect of the phone on the handlebar is eliminated by mathematically projecting the three-axis linear acceleration vector onto the normalized gravity vector. This will give an accurate vertical acceleration value from earth, regardless of the phone's position. High frequency sensor noise is smoothed by using a 5-sample moving average window. At the same time, CameraX takes frames of the road at 200 millisecond intervals (around 5 frames per second) and stores them in a circular buffer of 25 frames. This helps to maintain approximately 5 seconds of pre-impact road footage, which is available for visual verification of frames prior to physical impact.
[030] (p  |Heading2       ): Detection Layer
[031] (p  |BodyText       ): Detection is multi-stage: feature extraction using consensus voting, kinematic filtering and visual confirmation. First, impact features are extracted from the vertical acceleration of the system and are then compared with four threshold algorithms: Z-THRESH, Z-DIFF, STDEV(Z), and G-ZERO. An anomaly will only be reported as a candidate anomaly if at least two out of the four algorithms fire at the same time. Second, the candidate is passed through kinematic rejection gates to reject common driving maneuvers. The hard braking filter will ignore events where a vehicle's horizontal acceleration is greater than 4.5 m/s2 in an upright suspension. The turning filter is used to eliminate events when the angular yaw velocity is above 0.3 rad/s. Multiple entries for the same road defect will be prevented with a temporal "cooldown" of 1.5 seconds and a spatial "duplicate radius" of 15 meters within 3.0 seconds. Third, candidate events are confirmed visually on demand, with a light-weight model called YOLOv8n, through TensorFlow Lite. To avoid losing sensor samples, feature processing and vision inference are performed asynchronously and work on a background thread in Kotlin coroutines. The model takes a 640x640 RGB image as input and produces a single tensor with 8400 predictions from anchors, with four bounding box coordinates and a pothole confidence score. When none of the detection is more than 0.55 confidence, this is considered a visual false alarm and is rejected
[032] (p  |Heading2       ): Storage Layer
[033] (p  |None           ): Confirmed potholes are stored in a local Room database with a unique event identifier, timestamp, GPS coordinates, severity rating, impact magnitude, and a reference to the saved camera frame. In addition, SpotHole writes continuous telemetry files on an asynchronous I/O thread for offline evaluation: raw sensor readings (sensor_log.csv), candidate trigger decisions and rejection reasons (candidate_log.csv), and session metadata containing the vehicle type, mount type, phone model, and active threshold parameters

[035] (p  |Heading2       ): Presentation Layer
[036] (p  |None           ): The presentation layer uses osmdroid to render OpenStreetMap tiles showing detected potholes as color-coded markers based on severity (low, medium, and high). A live waveform component displays real-time vertical acceleration during test rides. Users can review logged ride sessions and export all database records and raw CSV telemetry files as a compressed ZIP archive using the native Android share system.
[037] (p  |Heading1       ): Methodology
[038] (p  |Heading2       ): Overview
[039] (p  |None           ): The detection process involves three stages: multi-heuristic consensus voting on inertial features, kinematic rejection filtering and on-demand camera verification. Only if a pothole event is detected at all three stages in succession will it be recorded as a confirmed event. Each threshold is configured with the motorcycle handlebar mount in mind, as it is a hard mount.
[040] (p  |Heading2       ): Signal Processing and Feature Extraction
[041] (p  |None           ): It is important to note that the service ignores sensor readings in the first 1.0 second of each session, to let the Android sensor fusion filters (gravity and linear acceleration filters) stabilize. The latest synchronized values obtained from the gyroscope, gravity and linear acceleration sensors are acquired on each callback of the accelerometer.
[042] (p  |None           ): To make measurements independent of the mounting angle of the smartphone, the 3-axis linear acceleration vector (a_lin) is projected onto the normalized gravity vector (g_hat) to calculate vertical acceleration (a_v):
[043] (p  |None           ): a_v = a_lin · g_hat,                       where g_hat = g / ||g||
[044] (p  |None           ): The vertical acceleration measured by the grav sensor is the true vertical acceleration, which is independent of the angle of the handlebar since it is dynamically following the true direction of the earth's pull. A five-sample moving average window smooths out high frequency (hardware) noise and creates a_v_smoothed.
[045] (p  |None           ): The feature extractor uses these signals to calculate three main detection metrics:
[046] (p  |None           ): The absolute difference between the current smoothed vertical value and the value of 10 samples before (about 148 milliseconds before, at the measured sampling rate of 67.7 Hz). This is to record fast compression of suspensions.
[047] (p  |None           ): RMS: Root means square of the current vertical acceleration (smoothing time constant of 0.33 seconds). AbsRMS: Absolute value of the current RMS vertical acceleration (smoothing time constant of 0.33 seconds).
[048] (p  |None           ): STDEV(Z): Standard deviation for a rolling window of the previous 20 unsmoothed vertical acceleration readings that represent surface roughness.
[049] (p  |None           ): Further, the pipeline calculates the sum of the absolute values of the raw accelerations along all axes (including gravity) (m), the raw horizontal acceleration (a_h), and the angular rate of yaw (gyroscope projected onto the vertical axis).
[050] (p  |Heading2       ): Heuristic Consensus Voting
[051] (p  |None           ): The first detection stage implements the four classical heuristics introduced by Mednis et al., re-tuned for two-wheeler dynamics:
[052] (p  |None           ): 
[053] (p  |None           ): Z-DIFF triggers when Z_diff is greater than or equal to 5.0 m/s².
[054] (p  |None           ): Z-THRESH triggers when Z_thresh is greater than or equal to 3.92 m/s² (0.40 g).
[055] (p  |None           ): STDEV(Z) triggers when the rolling standard deviation is greater than or equal to 5.0 m/s².
[056] (p  |None           ): G-ZERO triggers when total acceleration magnitude m drops below 2.5 m/s² for three consecutive samples, detecting momentary free-fall as the front wheel drops into a cavity.
[057] (p  |None           ): Only two or more of the four algorithms starting at the same time create a candidate shock. Samples that have less than two votes are eliminated.
[058] (p  |Heading2       ): Kinematic Rejection Filters
[059] (p  |None           ): Consensus voting techniques are used to remove false alarms due to rider movements (Table II) after the candidate passes the consensus voting. Hard Braking Gate: Discards events with a horizontal acceleration a_h above 4.5 m/s² and a vertical shock Z_diff below 2.5 m/s² to remove events with forward weight transfer under heavy braking. Turning Gate: Blocks lateral g-forces during fast cornering, by rejecting events with an absolute yaw rate above 0.3 rad/s. GYROSCOPE MAGNITUDE GATE: Filters out handling adjustments made by the vehicle when overall angular velocity is above 1.2 rad/s but Z_diff (vertical shock) is less than 2.5 m/s2. Cooldown and Spatial Deduplication: Implements 1.5-second cooldown after any confirmed event, suppressing other events that occur within 15 meters of the event within a 3.0-second window (using the Haversine formula). Speed Gate Note: GPS speed filter for events slower than 10kmph (2.77 m/s) is included in the codebase to prevent handling noise from pedestrians. This check is disabled in the evaluated build to test the system in a stationary condition and low-speed tests.
[060] (p  |Heading2       ): Camera Confirmation
[061] (p  |None           ): CameraX shoots road frames at 640x640 resolution with no more than 5 frames per second, with a minimum delay of 200 milliseconds and a 25-frame buffer.
[062] (p  |None           ): If an inertial candidate passes all rejection filters, then the service fetches all the buffered frames that have been captured in the last 3.0 seconds before the shock (up to 15 frames) and will then check them in reverse chronological order (newest first). The pre-processing of the frames is performed as a letterbox aspect ratio is preserved and the frame is also normalized between 0.0 and 1.0.
[063] (p  |None           ): This single-class YOLOv8n model uses 4 CPU threads to run on-device with the TensorFlow Lite runtime. This model generates one tensor containing 8400 candidate anchor predictions, all of which have 4 bounding box coordinates and one confidence score for pothole class. The event is visually detected on the first frame that any anchor has a pothole confidence level of 0.25 or greater. The app avoids non-maximum suppression (NMS) to avoid edge latency since all it needs is confirmation that there is a pothole. In case of an exception, inference is safely rejected.
[064] (p  |Heading2       ): Severity Classification and Event Recording
[065] (p  |None           ): The severity of confirmed potholes is classified based on vertical shock magnitude into High (>=9.0 m/s2), Medium (6.5-9.0 m/s2), and Low (<6.5 m/s2) categories, as configured in Table II.
[066] (p  |None           ): 
[067] (p  |None           ): The frame buffer is used with "speed adaptive lookback timing" to select the photographic artifact to be saved in retrospect for each event:
[068] (p  |None           ): t_lookback = clamp (8.0 / v, 0.3, 2.0) + 0.1 seconds \
[069] (p  |None           ): where v is the speed of the vehicle (m/s) measured by the GPS.
[070] (p  |None           ): This will aim at the image taken at the time the pothole was about 8 meters in front of the front tire. Confirmed events are documented including their UUID, timestamp, lat, long, severity value, impact value, and JPEG image path.

[086] (p  |Heading2       ): Data Quality and Leakage Analysis
[087] (p  |None           ): A cross-split perceptual hashing audit (with 16x16 grayscale perceptual hashes and Hamming distance threshold 4 or less) found 242 near-duplicate image pairs between the training and validation sets. Near duplicate cross-contamination occurs because both open-source collections adopted imagery from overlapping web sources, leading to some images being memorized from the training set. In order to create an uncompromised evaluation baseline, we report both standard validation metrics and synthetic perturbation benchmarks in Section VII. Furthermore, the validation set is built entirely from images that do not have any background (clean road) in them, thus the false-positive rate on clean asphalt cannot be checked on this benchmark alone.
[088] (p  |Heading2       ): Model Architecture and Training Configuration
[089] (p  |None           ): We chose the YOLOv8n (nano) detection model with 3,011,043 parameters and 8.19 GFLOPs at 640x644 resolution that offers a balance of edge inference speed and localization accuracy on the mobile platform.
[090] (p  |None           ): All training was done on a NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4050 Laptop GPU with 6 GB of VRAM. Two model configurations were trained: One 5 epoch baseline model, which is directly initialized from the official yolov8n.pt pre-trained model; and a 50-epoch model fine-tuned from the baseline checkpoint. The settings for training were deterministically set using random seed 0.

[093] (p  |Heading2       ): Detection Accuracy
[094] (p  |None           ): The two trained models YOLOv8n are validated on the 528 validation images that are usable, with a confidence threshold of 0.001 and an Intersection-over-Union (IoU) threshold of 0.6. Training for 50 epochs instead of 5 increased precision by 0.0839, recall by 0.0736, F1 score by 0.0784, mAP@0.5 by 0.0920, and mAP@0.5:0.95 by 0.1033.
[095] (p  |None           ): The final 50-epoch model achieves an mAP@0.5 of 0.7992 and an F1 score of 0.7564, but an mAP@0.5:0.95 of 0.5162. This difference means that the detector is able to be confident about the presence and approximate location of the potholes, but the overlap accuracy drops as the IoU gets higher, because the contours of a pothole are not always regular. Since just binary visual identification of an anomaly is enough for SpotHole, the most important operational metrics are mAP@0.5 and recall. The recall of 0.6986 suggests that around 30% of the potholes that are annotated are not detected at the standard overlap, which are mostly surface blemishes that are either far away or not sufficiently in contrast.

[102] (p  |Heading2       ): Impact of Input Aspect Ratio Resizing
[103] (p  |None           ): Comparing image pre-processing strategies for scaling arbitrary road frames to the model's 640x640 input resolution reveals that direct distortion causes a catastrophic 93.9% relative performance drop because stretching alters the geometric aspect ratios of pothole cavities, making them unrecognizable to the convolutional anchors. Consequently, SpotHole enforces letterbox aspect-ratio preservation within its CameraX pre-processing pipeline.

[114] (p  |None           ): A. Evaluation and Dataset Limitations
[115] (p  |None           ): First, every image in our validation set contains at least one pothole. While this allows us to test localization and recall, it means we cannot measure how often the camera model raises false alarms on clean, undamaged asphalt. The reported precision is therefore likely higher than what a rider will experience on normal roads.
[116] (p  |ListParagraph  ): 
[117] (p  |None           ): B. Method and Algorithm Limitations
[118] (p  |None           ): 
[119] (p  |None           ): Our detection thresholds are fixed numbers tuned specifically for a commuter motorcycle with a rigid handlebar mount. If the app is moved to a scooter, a bicycle, or a car, or even to a different mount that dampens vibrations differently, these thresholds will need to be readjusted.
[120] (p  |None           ): Lighting and vibration remain the hardest hurdles for the camera. In our tests, severe vibration blur cut detection accuracy by more than half (a 56% drop), and dim lighting dropped it by 0.260 mAP. Riding at night therefore depends almost entirely on streetlights or the phone's flashlight.
[121] (p  |Heading1       ): Conclusion
[122] (p  |None           ): This paper introduces SpotHole, a low-energy Android sensing framework designed to identify potholes using a motorcycle handlebar-mounted smartphone. To reduce the number of false positive detections while conserving battery power and avoiding processor overheating, the system has been developed with a two-part operational process. The first part involves continuous 100Hz inertial sensing with gravity projection that is independent of the device's orientation; consensus voting based upon the results of four different heuristic-based detection algorithms; and kinematic rejection filters. The second part is an on-demand visual verification check completed through the use of a small-scale one-class YOLOv8n detector.
